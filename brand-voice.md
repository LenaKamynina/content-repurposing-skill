## Voice vs. tone

Voice stays the same all the time. Tone changes depending on context — who you're addressing and their emotional state. Never use the same tone with someone who's scared or upset as you would with someone who's laughing.

## Voice

We speak like the experienced and compassionate business partner we wish we'd had. We treat every hopeful brand seriously — we educate without patronizing or confusing. We use offbeat humor and a conversational voice, playing with language to bring joy to work. We prefer the subtle over the noisy, the wry over the farcical. We don't take ourselves too seriously.

Four traits, always:

1. **Plainspoken.** We strip away hyperbolic language, upsells, and over-promises. Clarity above all — no fluffy metaphors, no cheap plays to emotion.
2. **Genuine.** We were a small business too. We relate to customers' challenges and speak to them in a familiar, warm, accessible way.
3. **Translators.** We demystify B2B-speak. Only experts can make what's difficult look easy — that's our job.
4. **Dry humor.** Straight-faced, subtle, a touch eccentric. Weird but not inappropriate, smart but not snobbish. We wink, we don't shout. Never condescending — we always bring the customer in on the joke.

## Tone

Usually informal, but clarity always beats entertainment. Read the room: is this reader relieved a campaign just went out, or confused and asking for help? Adjust tone to their emotional state.

Be funny when it's appropriate and comes naturally — never force a joke. Forced humor is worse than none. When in doubt, keep a straight face.

## Style tips

- **Active voice.** Avoid passive voice, except when specifically emphasizing the action over the subject (e.g., "Your account was flagged by our Abuse team").
- **No slang or jargon.** Plain English. If a technical term is necessary, define it briefly.
- **Write positively.** Use positive framing instead of negative ("To get a donut, stand in line" not "You can't get a donut if you don't stand in line").

## Grammar & mechanics basics

- Write for skimmers and full readers alike — group related ideas, use descriptive headers.
- Lead with the main point. Create a hierarchy of information.
- Be concise: short words, short sentences, no unnecessary modifiers.
- Be specific: cut vague language and fluff.
- Be consistent with the patterns in this guide.

## Guidelines

**Abbreviations/acronyms** — spell out on first use if the reader might not recognize it, then abbreviate afterward. Well-known ones (API, HTML) don't need spelling out.

**Capitalization** — sentence case or title case as appropriate; never capitalize random mid-sentence words like *website*, *internet*, *online*, *email*. Email addresses and URLs are always lowercase.

**Contractions** — encouraged; they read as informal and friendly.

**Emoji** — fine in small, deliberate doses. Not a default.

**Numbers** — spell out only when starting a sentence; numerals otherwise, including ordinals ("1st place"). Commas for numbers over 3 digits (1,000). Abbreviate large numbers only under space constraints (1k, 150k).

**Percentages** — use the % symbol, never "percent."

**Ranges** — hyphen, no spaces ("20-30 days").

**Punctuation essentials:**
- Serial (Oxford) comma always.
- Em dash (—) for asides, no spaces around it — never a hyphen or double-hyphen standing in for it.
- One space between sentences.
- Exclamation points: sparingly, never doubled, never in failure/alert messaging.
- Semicolons: avoid — use an em dash or split into two sentences instead.
- Ampersands: only when part of an official company/brand name.

**Pronouns** — "they/them/their" as a singular pronoun when gender is unknown or irrelevant. Never "one" as a pronoun.

**Text formatting** — italics for titles of long works or emphasis, and for citing in-app UI labels in instructions (e.g., "click *Send*"). No underline. Never stack italic + bold + caps + underline. Left-align always.

## Writing about Mailchimp

- Trade name: **Mailchimp** (capital M, lowercase c). Legal entity name "The Rocket Science Group, LLC" only in legal documents/contracts.
- Refer to Mailchimp as **"we,"** never "it."
- Capitalize branded terms (Mailchimp Presents) and pricing plan names (Premium, Standard, Essentials, Free) to distinguish from generic use.
- Don't capitalize generic product/feature terms: *email*, *landing pages*, *templates*, *Mailchimp's mobile app*, *Essentials plan* (plan names still capitalized per above).

## Writing about other companies

Use their own name for themselves and their products (iPad, YouTube, Yahoo!). Refer to another company/product as **"it,"** not "they."

## Text formatting

See Punctuation and Grammar & mechanics above — the core rules that matter most when repurposing for social/newsletter: em dash not double-hyphen, serial comma, single space between sentences, sparing/never-doubled exclamation points, positive framing, active voice.

## Words to avoid

Never use these — swap in plain, specific language instead:

- automagical
- funnel, incentivize, leverage, disruption, thought leader, learnings, and other fluffy corporate terms
- internets, interwebs, or any variation of "internet" other than "internet"
- ninja, rockstar, wizard, unicorn (unless referring to a literal one)
- young, old, elderly, or any word describing a person's age
- crushing it, killing it
- crazy, insane, or similar words describing people
- best-in-breed
- Silicon Valley clichés: rise and grind, disruptor/disruption
- activation (when referring to event presence)
- blacklist, whitelist, grandfathered, slave, master, deaf, blind, and any other racist or ableist terms
